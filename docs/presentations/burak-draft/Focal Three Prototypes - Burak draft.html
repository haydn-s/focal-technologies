The problem

Convenient or thorough: pick one

2.2 billion

people live with a vision impairment. At least 1 billion of those cases were preventable or not yet addressed.

Access is hard

Even a basic check means a clinic visit and a wait.

Change is hard to see

One snapshot can’t show a trend, and kids memorize home charts.

Online tests aren’t trusted

No credible source behind them, and a number with no next step.

Source: WHO, World report on vision (2019)

How our thinking changed

What we knew then vs. now

When we started

  • The barrier is access: exams are hard to book.
  • People need a more accurate test.
  • Big institutional buyers: DMVs, airlines, pilots.

After 10 interviews

  • Avoidance isn’t only logistics: “maybe I’m fine” feels safer.
  • Urgency comes from outside: a prescription, a school letter, a DMV date.
  • Trust and a next step matter more than accuracy.
  • Pilots dropped. Licensing agencies stay a hypothesis.

10 interviews · 4 personas · 5 in person, 5 virtual (AI-simulated personas)

Prototype A · Routine-Check Riley

Focal Monitor

Hypothesis

Adults who notice their vision slipping will find ongoing tracking more useful than a single test result.

Success signal (proposed)

3 of 5 Riley-like testers state a format preference and explain why.

The core flow

  1. An eight-letter tumbling-E check
  2. Today’s result, shown on its own
  3. The same result on a seven-week trend (illustrative history)
  4. Neutral feedback: which view helped, and would you return?

Why we believe it

Riley goes by feel, with “no baseline to compare against.” A single score can’t show change.

Trigger: a prescription about to expire · Built by Qingyi and Fan

5

Prototype B · Parent Pat

Focal Family

Hypothesis

Parents will finish a short picture game with their child, then use what they noticed to pick a next step.

Success signal (proposed)

4 of 5 parents finish unaided; 3 of 5 prepare an appointment or school contact.

The core flow

  1. Practice, then six large matching pictures
  2. The parent notes what they’ve noticed
  3. The parent picks a next step and prepares questions
  4. A summary to bring along. No score, by design.

Why we believe it

Kids memorize home charts, and Pat wants “a reasonable reason to get it checked.”

Trigger: a school screening letter · Built by Haydn

6

Prototype C · Anxious Applicant Alex

DMV Pre-Check

Hypothesis

People with a DMV eye test coming up will prefer, and act on, a check that answers “Will I pass my state’s test?”

Success signal (proposed)

60% with a DMV visit coming up would use it; 50% told to see a doctor first would book before the DMV.

The core flow

  1. Pick your state and see its real rule
  2. Size the letters to your screen with any ID card
  3. Read 14 letters, six at the line that decides
  4. Get a verdict and a next-step plan

Why we believe it

An online test gave Alex “20/30,” and he “didn’t even know if that’s a pass or a fail.”

Trigger: a DMV vision screening coming up · Built by Burak
