# Interactive prototype assignment

Assignment information supplied for the MENG 570 prototype work. The original brief follows.

Your team will take the problem statement you defined at the start of the semester and build three interactive prototypes of your solution (product). Each prototype should address one of the User Personas you defined earlier for your product.  This not to be mockups or slide decks. Three real, interactive, clickable prototypes that someone can actually use.

What This Assignment Is About

The goal of this assignment is to emphasize the fact that most business and many product decisions start as hypotheses - that need to be tested.  In this case, your "hypothesis" could be construed as "can we effectively target all user personas with one product approach" or if not, "which of these user personas should we focus on first?"  We are emphasizing how to develop a product strategy grounded in evidence rather than assumptions. You already have a company name, a logo, and a problem statement. Now it is time to build something and find out whether your instincts and customer discovery analysis about the solution are correct.

As you have defined 3 user personas already, our experiment will be to see if we can deliver an interactive prototype that supports all 3.  It would probably be easier to develop a prototype for just one persona at a time - so this is the challenge. (If it turns out that all of your personas are relatively easily supported by a single prototype, then you could (for instance) vary your prototype instead by the types of functionality you offer.  It would still be a valid test to put these prototypes in front of customers representing all of your users to see which functional approaches have the highest resonance across the most customers.)

I want you to build 3 unique prototypes that each embody a different hypothesis.  You'll have a lot of leeway in defining the hypotheses you want to test; but you will need to define each hypothesis well enough so that we can all determine how well your prototype tests that hypothesis.

You are building three versions because that is how real product decisions get made. When a PM is not sure whether users want approach A or approach B, they do not debate it in a meeting — they prototype both and let users tell them. That is exactly what you will do. Your prototypes should represent genuinely different approaches to solving the same problem. They should not be the same product with a different color scheme. They should reflect different hypotheses about what your users need and how best to serve them.

What You Need to Build

Use Claude Code, v0, Replit, Cursor, or any AI-native coding tool of your choice to build prototypes. Each version should be functional and interactive — a real person must be able to open them, navigate them, and understand what the product does without you explaining it first. If your prototype requires a live demo to make sense, it is not ready. Build until it speaks for itself.  (However, these are not intended to be fully functional product offerings!  These are just prototypes of some aspect of the full offering that you are choosing to experiment with.)

The prototypes must be live and accessible via a shared link by the time you present in class.

The In-Class Presentation

Your presentation should be concise, structured, and demo-forward. Here is exactly what it needs to cover:

The first part is your problem and approach. Remind the class who you are building for, what problem you identified at the start of the semester, and how your thinking about that problem has evolved since then. What did you know when you started, and what do you know now? This should take no more than two minutes.

The second part is your product and the three versions. Walk the class through what you built. Explain the core feature or flow that each prototype addresses - this is the aspect of an overall product offering that you are experimenting with.  Clearly articulate what is different between Versions A, B and C. Specifically - What hypothesis does each version test? What are you trying to learn from the comparison? This should take about two minutes per hypothesis.

The third part is the live demo. Show each prototype working in real time. Do not narrate every click — let the product do the talking. You have approximately two minutes per prototype. The demos should be clean and rehearsed. If something breaks during the demo, handle it professionally and move on.

The fourth part is your justification and strategy. After the demos, explain why you built it this way. What prioritization decisions did you make and why? What did you cut, and was that the right call? What does building these three versions tell you about the direction you want to take the product? This is where you demonstrate PM thinking — not just that you built something, but that you understood why you built it and what hypotheses you were testing.

The Class Vote

After your demos, the class votes. Every other team votes on which of your prototypes they would actually use. This is your A/B test. The result is real feedback from a room full of people who just saw each version with fresh eyes.

You do not need to agree with the vote. But you do need to respond to it. After the results are announced, your team with move forward with that version for the remaining semester work - specifically leading up to the final Investor Pitch presentation in week 11.  A thoughtful disagreement with the class is just as valid as agreement, as long as you can defend it with logic and evidence.  Thus, if you specifically disagree with the class vote, you may approach the teach team over the next several days to argue for a version of the prototype your team prefers.  The teach team will prefer to proceed with the class voted option, but will entertain well reasoned arguments from the team.

How You Will Be Graded

Your grade is based on four things.

Your presentation will be evaluated on clarity, structure, and how well you communicate the PM reasoning behind your decisions. A polished slide deck with no substance will not score well. A rough but honest walk-through of your thinking will.

Your working prototypes will be evaluated on whether they function as intended, whether they are genuinely distinct from each other, and whether someone could use them without a guided tour. Each prototype must work. A broken prototype is not a prototype.

Your ideas and execution will be evaluated on the quality of your problem framing, the creativity and clarity of your approaches, and how well the prototypes reflect deliberate product decisions rather than whatever was easiest to build.

Your understanding of the problem will be evaluated on how deeply you have engaged with the users you are building for. Do your prototypes reflect real insight into the problem you defined at the start of the semester? Does your justification show that you understand not just what you built but why it matters?

A Few Things to Keep in Mind

Start with the Feature Scope Card from the in-class activity. Apply the same prioritization thinking — value, effort, complexity, success signal — to decide what each prototype will and will not include. Scope is your friend. A tight, focused prototype that does one thing well will outperform a sprawling one that tries to do everything.

Use AI tools to build fast, but direct them with intention. Log at least five prompts per prototype as you work — capture what you asked for and what you were trying to learn. The log is part of your deliverable and part of how we assess your PM thinking.

Rehearse your demo. Twice. Things go wrong during live demos and the teams that handle it gracefully are the ones who practiced enough to stay calm when it happens.

The class vote is data, not a verdict. You are not trying to win a popularity contest. You are trying to learn something real about your product. Treat the vote that way.

Deliverables

Submit the following to Canvas before your presentation day:

A shared link to each Prototype, all fully functional and accessible. Your presentation slides or document, including your problem statement, approach, prototype descriptions, hypotheses and justification.

You have been thinking about this problem since the start of the semester. You have a team, a name, and a direction. This is the week you find out if your hypothesis holds up when something real is in front of real people. Build something honest, demo it with confidence, and let the feedback make you sharper.
